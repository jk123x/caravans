## Battery

> **TL;DR: Buy lithium. Not AGM, not gel. Lithium. It costs more upfront but it lasts years longer and you get way more usable power from it. A 200Ah lithium battery is the sweet spot for most people.**

The battery is your fuel tank. It's the most important part of the system, and the good news is the decision is easy.

You'll see two types recommended: AGM and lithium. People will tell you AGM is fine if you're on a budget. Here's the thing: an AGM battery that says "200Ah" on the label only gives you about 100Ah of usable power — you can only use half of it. A lithium battery that says "200Ah" gives you 160-200Ah of usable power.

So you'd need two AGMs to match one lithium, and those AGMs will weigh twice as much and need replacing in 3-5 years. The lithium lasts 10+.

**The most common beginner mistake is buying AGM now and upgrading to lithium a year later.** That's $400-$800 spent on batteries that end up in the bin. Buy lithium from the start and you're done.

**What to buy by tier:**
- Weekender: 100-200Ah lithium
- Tourer: 200Ah lithium (one battery does the job)
- Full-timer: 300-400Ah lithium

Make sure it has a built-in BMS (Battery Management System) — this manages the charging automatically. All the brands we recommend include one.

## Solar Panels

> **TL;DR: Get fixed panels on the roof PLUS a portable folding panel. The roof panels do the day-to-day work; the portable one is your backup for when the roof is in shade. Go monocrystalline — it's the standard now.**

Two things to know about solar panels:

**Fixed panels** bolt to your roof and work all day without you doing anything. The downside: they're flat on the roof, so they're not angled toward the sun, and they get hot (which reduces how much power they make). They also can't avoid shade from your air conditioner or antenna.

**Portable panels** fold out and sit on the ground at camp. You can angle them at the sun and put them wherever the shade isn't. Downsides: you have to set them up and pack them away, and keep an eye on them.

Most people get both. Fixed for daily passive charging, portable for the boost.

**One thing worth knowing:** a "400 watt" panel doesn't actually produce 400 watts. That rating is from a perfect lab test. On your roof, in real Australian conditions, expect about 50-70% of what the label says. A 400W setup might give you 200-280W on a good day. That's completely normal — and we've already accounted for it. When this guide says "get 400W of panels," the real-world performance is baked in.

**What to buy by tier:**
- Weekender: 200W fixed
- Tourer: 300-400W fixed + 160-200W portable
- Full-timer: 400-600W fixed + 200W+ portable

## Charge Controller

> **TL;DR: If you're a Weekender with under 200W of solar, a PWM controller is fine. Everyone else, get an MPPT controller — it squeezes more power from your panels. Buy one rated about 1/3 of your total solar wattage in amps (e.g. 400W solar → 30-40A controller).**

This sits between your panels and your battery. It manages the charging. You buy the right size once and don't think about it again.

There are two types: PWM and MPPT. MPPT squeezes about 15-30% more power from your panels. On a bigger system, that difference adds up, so it's worth the upgrade.

**What to buy by tier:**
- Weekender: 20A PWM or MPPT (either is fine)
- Tourer: 30-40A MPPT
- Full-timer: 40-50A MPPT

## DC-DC Charger

> **TL;DR: This charges your battery from the car while you drive. It's not optional. A few hours of driving puts a big chunk of power back in your battery. Buy a REDARC BCDC — it's the standard in Australia for a reason.**

Modern cars have "smart alternators" that don't play nicely with a direct battery connection. A DC-DC charger sorts this out — it takes power from the car and feeds it to your caravan battery at the right rate.

This is your second biggest charging source after solar. On a day when you drive 3-4 hours, a 40A DC-DC charger can put 120-160Ah back in your battery. That's a huge amount.

**What to buy by tier:**
- Weekender: 20A DC-DC charger
- Tourer: 40A DC-DC charger
- Full-timer: 40-60A DC-DC charger

## Inverter

> **TL;DR: Get a pure sine wave inverter. NEVER buy a "modified sine wave" — it can damage your stuff. Size it for the biggest thing you'll plug into it. Weekender: 1000W. Tourer: 2000W. Full-timer: 3000W.**

Your battery runs on 12 volts. Your laptop charger, microwave, and hair dryer need 240 volts (same as your house). The inverter converts it.

The only decision: how big. Think about the biggest single appliance you'll want to run:
- Just phones and laptops? 1000W is plenty.
- Microwave or toaster? You need 2000W.
- Hair dryer or multiple big appliances? 3000W.

**Always buy pure sine wave.** The cheap alternative is "modified sine wave" — it saves you $50-$100 but produces rough power that doesn't play nicely with laptops and chargers. Pure sine wave is identical to what comes out of your wall at home. Everything just works.

## Battery Monitor

> **TL;DR: Buy a Victron SmartShunt ($130-$160). It shows your battery level on your phone via Bluetooth. Without a monitor, you're driving without a fuel gauge — you'll wear out your battery without realising it.**

This is your fuel gauge. Without it you're guessing how much power you've got left, and guessing means you'll run things down further than you should — which wears out the battery faster over time.

The Victron SmartShunt is the one to get. About $130-$160, installs on the battery cable, and talks to your phone over Bluetooth. It tells you exactly what percentage you're at, how fast you're using power, and how long until it's full. Check your battery level from bed.
